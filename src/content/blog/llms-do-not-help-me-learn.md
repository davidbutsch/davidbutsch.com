I have committed to this idea of pursuing knowledge in all ways possible. The work I do should result in new skills I did not have before. Knowledge is important to me solely because I desire to understand the world. Then, LLMs exist in this state of limbo. On one hand, I understand their usefulness in my studies and work, and I cannot say my reliance on these tools is entirely negative. There are significant productivity benefits in using LLMs to tackle hard problems or explain non-trivial concepts. Namely, LLMs let me move faster, and faster is good.

But faster is not everything I want. I truly desire to learn, and LLMs have proven to be something of a learning hindrance. We learn new skills by struggling, but we use LLMs to reduce struggle. One of the more popular applications of language models is the restructuring of information. I think this is a largely destructive practice at scale. We understand excessive task delegation can hurt our abilities, but don’t seem to recognize content digestion as a task in itself. I would consider it among the most important tasks. Struggling to shape content in a way that fits our mind is an exceedingly effective way to learn.

## The modern episteme

I am aware these tools degrade my abilities to reason and implement, but I struggle to justify the old ways of doing work. For example, I love programming, but now I see it as an inefficiency. I believe a lot of us do this, and we do this because it is the modern infatuation. I was speaking with a friend who introduced me to this idea of an episteme. I understand it to be some hidden social agreement to pursue one facet of reality in science and research. The idea comes from French philosopher Michel Foucault, who wrote about two epistemes: the similarities between objects, and the differences between objects. Each of these epistemes existed in different times, and my friend made the claim that optimization was the modern-day episteme. So societies have become rather obsessed with optimizing our lives. I think we all see how this rings true. My friend and I spoke about restaurant buildings losing unique quirks to optimize the turning over of ownership from one franchise to the next. Someone able and willing to do skilled work, but who instead opts to delegate that work to an LLM, is certainly appealing to the modern episteme.

We are so fascinated with this idea of efficiency that we unknowingly forfeit certain inefficiencies necessary to learn. I’ll continue with the importance of manual information ingestion. Imagine you are a computer system with ephemeral memory and permanent disk storage. The thing about collecting information without an LLM is that you encounter much information which is not directly relevant to the problem you are trying to solve. When processing content in this manner, you are exposed to so much data that you must choose what to discard and what to keep. I believe we are more prone to save information, because we are often unsure about what information is even relevant to begin with.

On the other hand, LLMs produce only the most relevant information to solve a problem. This means we spend less time working through presently useless data. However, it also means we don’t have to make that critical choice to save information. In essence, information generated by an LLM lives in our ephemeral memory system. It exists as an illusion of knowledge, but does not persist like true knowledge. We have ready access at the moment, but never actually retain it. Retaining information requires energy and time, which does not make for timely problem solving.

## Why pursue knowledge?

So knowledge gets in the way of solving the immediate problem. Then, how can anyone say that knowledge is still good or even necessary? If I choose to reject the optimization episteme, I may still defend pursuing knowledge. But what if I choose to embrace optimization?

I may give the impression that optimization is an inherently negative thing. I don’t think that way. All I claim is that optimization permeates society. Even if I did view this supposed episteme in a negative light, I am not even sure it is something we can willingly change. Rather, it may be a natural occurrence which should change over time. So I choose to embrace this reality. Part of this embrace is considering which skills are no longer necessary. So, I ask whether knowledge is still important. I want knowledge, but I may not need it.

I’ve had to sit on this question for a little while, and I believe I have found something of a personal resolve. I believe that if knowledge is actually important, then I should be able to justify it within the lens of optimization. If optimization is the modern episteme, then to be productive is to be efficient.

## Personal justification for knowledge

To justify knowledge, I reflect on my experience learning the Linux shell and command system. My reality is simple: I can learn Linux commands by spending more time with them (or really, by spending more time thinking about them). I use Fedora as my daily driver. I also prefer the terminal as my primary interface with the filesystem. These two let me comfortably say I use Linux commands on a regular basis, but I do not think about them very often. Instead, I use an LLM to refresh me on the commands I need to use. I give it a goal, it gives me a command. It’s quick, and could even be viewed as optimal. I don’t think it is. The reality is that these are very trivial and frequently visited tasks. An optimal system keeps temporally relevant information nearby, like a processor’s cache. So, when I interact with my system’s shell, delegating command authorship to an LLM is the equivalent of using a hard disk as system memory. I use the slower device while processing, all to save time loading information into the proper device. Short term gain, long term loss. Then, I claim that knowledge applied to frequented tasks bears real efficiency, and that knowledge is a justifiable venture in this scenario.

But this is really only true if it is important that the person is still in the loop. After all, tools do exist which allow for complete automation of system interfacing. I struggle with this. The discomforting reality is that LLMs are more efficient than people in generating content. So this is something I do not have an answer for. I have opinions, but cannot tell the future.

## Informational innovation

I think a fundamental question is whether or not LLMs have some implicit bottleneck that should prevent them from achieving true informational innovation. I look at informational innovation as some form of acquired knowledge which exists beyond the space of human attained knowledge. I believe this to be the goal of OpenAI, Anthropic, and the other competing labs. To be honest, even this may be an unnecessary qualifier to cut humans out of the loop. If LLMs continue to demonstrate proficiency in the human knowledge space, that proficiency may be enough to replace all but the most high-achieving human researchers. Perhaps we can look at this human knowledge space as being only sparsely explored. If LLMs are able to effectively explore that space, they will certainly outpace human knowledge. I also think we have seen evidence of this capability with the release of OpenAI’s mathematics papers, though I don't understand enough math to speak on the integrity of their findings.

Maybe to drive the point home, I’ll clarify what I mean by the human knowledge space and informational innovation. If we look at existing knowledge tools as something like components of a basis, then the span of that basis is what I call the human knowledge space. To discover a new knowledge tool which is independent from the rest would be the equivalent of finding a new, higher dimensional knowledge space. This act is what I call informational innovation. If LLMs are incapable of informational innovation, but are proficient in the existing human knowledge space, then they would be able to combine existing knowledge tools and explore the space much more completely than present human efforts.

## Dopamine

Coming back to learning strategy, I want to talk about dopamine as a tool. In particular, dopamine is a tool which lets me get things done I would otherwise not want to do. Tools like dopamine are powerful, but when misused can be very destructive. I look at LLMs as a misuse of dopamine.

Delayed gratification is really the entire strategy. By allowing yourself to sulk through unruly work, hitting that checkpoint can give you the dopamine high necessary to push through the next down period. It’s the heartbeat of productivity, one which LLMs completely sidestep. You get the result, you do little work. This environment changes how you think, and what you expect.

I believe it is largely impossible to understand a concept until you do it yourself. That is why I choose to do new things the hard way first. If I chose to delegate something new to an LLM, I would gain this unrealistic expectation for what is possible. Then, it becomes much more difficult to sit through that manual learning process in the future. Your expectation for immediate results dismantles any desire to gain meaningful knowledge. It’s the definition of a trap: easy to fall in, hard to get out.

It’s also much like doom scrolling, especially when you cut yourself out of the loop. Think about agentic tools. The content being generated by your LLM is meaningless to you. You don’t care what it says about the problem, you just care that the problem is resolved. Your mind is on autopilot and exerts no energy to make decisions. It feels like productivity, but it is not. In reality, it becomes a handicap. It alters your mind, making real work and growth feel impossible.

## How to learn in the AI era

In the past few days, I’ve made an intentional effort to do things the old way. Not to the extent that I get bogged down by irrelevant and frankly unhelpful problems, but in a way that forces me to engage with the content I am reading or writing. For example, disabling the AI overview in Google search (chrome extension) was a wonderful choice that made real information ingestion attainable again. When I want to use an LLM, I have autonomy over that choice. Otherwise, I read documentation, browse forums, and explore information to address whatever I'm working on. This lets me collect so much additional context that oftentimes I am able to reason through other problems by simply considering what I have already read.

I want to place some amount of emphasis on this ability to reason through problems. Before LLMs, answers were expensive. They were expensive to find, or they were expensive to derive. Generally, the more niche a problem, the more difficult it was to find an answer for it. Hence, we were required to spend time thinking about our problems. Today, we are conditioned to look towards LLMs in search of answers. The challenge is to resist that immediate urge. As with all things, it’s a balancing act. Arbitrary errors with very little signal may not be worth investigating. Or maybe configuration boilerplating is not your cup of tea. Certainly some things are more valuable to me than they are to you. It's necessary for you to identify those things. Recognize why they are valuable, and how they relate to your broader interests. Then, intentionally spend time with those questions. It’s the only way to learn, and it will set you apart from people who turn blindly towards or away from the AI revolution.
